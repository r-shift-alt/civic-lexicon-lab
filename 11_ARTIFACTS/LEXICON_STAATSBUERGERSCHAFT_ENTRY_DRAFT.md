## User Job Check

- What real wording, translation, or framing decision is this entry meant to help with?
  - It helps a bilingual political writer, editor, or translator decide when `Staatsbürgerschaft` should be translated directly as `citizenship` and when a nearby discussion is actually about civic participation, public spirit, or democratic formation rather than legal membership.
- Why is direct translation risky or incomplete here?
  - Because `citizenship` is often exactly right for legal status, but English also uses it for school-subject, civic-participation, and responsible-public-conduct contexts that do not map cleanly back onto German `Staatsbürgerschaft`.
- What kind of user would most likely need this entry?
  - Translation-adjacent political communicators, policy writers, editors, researchers, and journalists working between German and English on migration, democracy, public education, or civic discourse.

## Core Meaning Block

### Source-Language Context
- Concise working definition in source-language political context:
  - In German public and policy language, `Staatsbürgerschaft` commonly names the formal legal relationship between a person and a state, including the rights and duties attached to membership.

### Target-Language Context
- Concise working definition in target-language political context:
  - In English public and policy language, `citizenship` can name formal membership in a state, but it is also widely used for civic participation, citizenship education, and the formation of responsible members of society.

### Overlap Summary
- What is genuinely shared across both terms?
  - Both terms can refer to formal membership in a state, especially in contexts involving naturalization, legal status, eligibility, and the rights-and-duties bundle attached to belonging.

### Mismatch Summary
- What differs in baggage, institutional meaning, moral tone, or common usage?
  - The main mismatch is one of semantic range. German `Staatsbürgerschaft` stays relatively tight around legal membership and the state-person relationship. English `citizenship` can do that too, but it also expands more easily into civic ethos, democratic participation, and public-responsibility language.

## Framing Block

- Why is the term politically contested?
  - Because debates about citizenship often mix legal inclusion, national belonging, democratic duty, migration policy, and public expectations about how a member of the polity should behave.
- What framing baggage is common in German usage?
  - Legal status, nationality and naturalization questions, the rights-and-protections relationship between state and person, and the duties tied to formal membership.
- What framing baggage is common in English usage?
  - Legal status on one side, but also citizenship education, active participation, democratic literacy, and ideals of responsible conduct on the other.
- When is literal translation safe?
  - When the German source is clearly about legal membership, nationality law, naturalization, passport eligibility, or the rights-and-duties relationship attached to state membership.
- When is literal translation misleading?
  - When the surrounding discussion is really about civic-mindedness, common-good orientation, democratic participation, or public virtue and English happens to use `citizenship` as a broader civic label.
- Is there a false-equivalence warning that should be stated explicitly?
  - Yes. `Citizenship` is usually the correct rendering for `Staatsbürgerschaft` in legal-status contexts, but English `citizenship` should not be treated as proof that the German source is also talking about civic ethos, participation, or public spirit.

## Guidance Block

- Recommended rendering in policy context:
  - Use `citizenship` directly when the source is about legal membership, naturalization, formal rights, or state protection. If the legal-status meaning needs to be kept especially clear, use a restoring phrase such as `formal citizenship` or `citizenship status`.
- Recommended rendering in media or public-commentary context:
  - Use `citizenship` for reporting on nationality law, status disputes, and membership questions. If the English discussion drifts into civic-participation language, add a brief clarification or switch to a civic term rather than forcing everything into `Staatsbürgerschaft`.
- When should the original term be preserved and glossed?
  - Preserve `Staatsbürgerschaft` and add a gloss when the distinction between legal status and broader civic language is itself the point, or when a text compares German status language with English civic-language usage.
- When is paraphrase better than direct translation?
  - When the source or target text is really about civic-mindedness, public spirit, democratic participation, or citizenship education rather than about the formal legal bond between person and state.
- Reusable warning sentence for writers or translators:
  - If the German source is about legal membership in the state, `citizenship` is usually the right rendering; if the surrounding argument is really about civic-mindedness, public spirit, or democratic participation, do not assume `Staatsbürgerschaft` is still the best German match just because English keeps using `citizenship`.
