## User-Job Note

This entry is meant to help a bilingual political writer, editor, or translator decide whether a German use of `Solidarität` should be rendered directly as `solidarity` or glossed more contextually when the German source is invoking institutional social cohesion, labor-linked collective protection, or welfare-state obligation rather than movement rhetoric alone.

The likely user error is to assume that `solidarity` always carries the same practical weight in English that `Solidarität` can carry in German public language, especially when the German use leans on organized labor or social-state embeddedness.

## Provisional Mismatch Hypothesis

`Solidarität` and `solidarity` overlap strongly at the level of mutual support and collective obligation, but English `solidarity` often sounds more activist, mobilizing, or slogan-like than German `Solidarität` does in institutional or labor-embedded usage.

## Drafting Claim Check

### What seems genuinely shared
Both `Solidarität` and `solidarity` can name collective support, mutual obligation, and willingness to bear costs with or for others, especially in labor, crisis, or public-interest contexts.

### What seems mismatched
German `Solidarität` appears more comfortable inside organized labor and social-cohesion language that borders on institutional obligation, while English `solidarity` more often sounds like an explicit act of alignment, support, or mobilization.

### What advice a writer or translator may need
Use `solidarity` directly when the German source is clearly mobilizing, labor-forward, or moral-political in tone. Add a gloss or tighten the sentence when `Solidarität` is doing quieter work around social cohesion, social protection, or institutionalized mutual obligation that English `solidarity` may render too slogan-like or too thin.

## Drafting Warning to Carry Forward

Do not write the entry as if the problem were that `solidarity` is wrong.
The problem is narrower: `solidarity` is often close, but in some German policy or labor-adjacent uses it under-signals the routine institutional and social-obligation layer that `Solidarität` can carry.

## Candidate Reusable Warning Sentence

If the German source uses `Solidarität` to justify social protection, collective burden-sharing, or union-linked cohesion, plain `solidarity` may be accurate but still too movement-coded unless the surrounding sentence restores the institutional context.
