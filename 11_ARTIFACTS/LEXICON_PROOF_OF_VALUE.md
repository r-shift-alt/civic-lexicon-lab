# Lexicon Proof of Value

## Purpose

This note defines what would count as encouraging evidence that the German-English lexicon solves a real user problem rather than only sounding plausible in internal planning.

The goal is not to prove broad market demand in version one. The goal is to define a disciplined early threshold for continuing, revising, or killing the lexicon as the lead first artifact.

## What Must Be Tested

The first review cycle should test four questions:

1. Do target or proxy users recognize the problem as real?
2. Do they find at least some of the entry guidance practically useful?
3. Do the entries help them make a better wording, translation, or framing decision?
4. Can the artifact remain bounded enough to maintain without becoming a research sink?

## Minimum Review Object

The first proof-of-value review should not wait for all 8 entries.

Minimum review object:
- 3 draft entries of reasonably high quality;
- each entry must include all core fields from the MVP scope;
- each entry must contain at least one concrete translation warning and one recommendation;
- at least 2 of the 3 entries should come from high-friction concepts rather than easy cases.

Recommended first 3 entries:
- Solidarität / Solidarity
- soziale Marktwirtschaft / social market economy
- Staatsbürgerschaft / citizenship, with explicit note on when Bürgersinn is the relevant civic term instead

These are useful because they mix value language, institutional language, and false-equivalence risk.

## Minimum Reviewer Profile

The first review cycle does not require ideal end users, but reviewers should be close enough to the user job that their judgment is informative.

Minimum acceptable reviewer types:
- bilingual editor or writer working across German and English;
- policy or communications generalist who has translated or adapted arguments across those languages;
- researcher, campaign staffer, or policy aide who regularly explains contested concepts to mixed audiences.

Avoid using only reviewers who are:
- purely academic specialists with no practical writing or translation use case;
- politically interested but not engaged in actual wording decisions;
- internal project participants only.

## Success Threshold

The lexicon should count as encouraging if the first review cycle produces all of the following:

1. Problem recognition threshold
- at least 2 reviewers say the problem is real and recurring rather than niche or contrived.

2. Usefulness threshold
- at least 2 reviewers identify at least 2 concrete parts of the entries they would actually reuse, cite, adapt, or keep nearby during writing or translation work.

3. Decision-improvement threshold
- at least 2 reviewers report that an entry changed, clarified, or improved how they would translate, gloss, or frame at least one term.

4. Specificity threshold
- reviewers can point to what exactly helped them: warning, context note, recommendation, example, or false-equivalence note.
- vague praise does not count as proof of value.

5. Maintainability threshold
- drafting the first 3 entries does not require uncontrolled expansion into adjacent theory, historiography, or large corpus work.
- if the team can finish 3 good entries with bounded manual effort, the artifact still looks viable.

## Strong Positive Signal

The lexicon should count as strongly promising if:
- a reviewer says they would have wanted this in a real workflow they already perform;
- a reviewer explicitly prefers the entry over a generic dictionary or ordinary translation intuition for at least one concept;
- a reviewer asks for more entries in the same format;
- the same format appears reusable across multiple concepts without major redesign.

## Weak but Acceptable Signal

The lexicon may still be worth continuing if feedback is mixed but shows:
- the problem is real;
- the current format is imperfect but salvageable;
- users value the warnings and examples even if the prose is still too dense;
- the artifact needs narrowing, not abandonment.

This would justify one iteration, not indefinite refinement.

## Failure Signals

The lexicon should be reconsidered or demoted if several of the following appear:

1. Problem failure
- reviewers do not recognize the problem as important;
- reviewers see the problem as too rare to justify a dedicated artifact.

2. Utility failure
- reviewers say the entries are interesting but not practically usable during real work;
- reviewers cannot identify any sentence, warning, or recommendation they would reuse.

3. Format failure
- reviewers need too much explanation to understand how to use an entry;
- the entry format reads like internal analysis rather than a working aid.

4. Scope failure
- each entry requires excessive side research to avoid feeling irresponsible;
- the concept set constantly expands because one term cannot be treated without five others.

5. Maintenance failure
- completing even 3 entries at acceptable quality already feels too expensive relative to likely value.

## Continue / Revise / Kill Rule

Continue as lead artifact if:
- the success threshold is met;
- no major scope or maintenance failure appears;
- the format seems reusable across at least several of the 8 concepts.

Revise but continue cautiously if:
- the problem is validated;
- at least some practical utility appears;
- but format, density, or sourcing rules need adjustment.

Kill or demote as lead artifact if:
- the problem is weakly recognized;
- practical utility is not visible;
- or the first 3 entries already show chronic scope drift.

If killed or demoted, switch attention to the campaign knowledge transfer template as the fallback first artifact.

## Review Questions

Each reviewer should be asked questions like:
- Would this have helped you in a real wording, translation, or framing decision?
- Which part of the entry was actually useful?
- What would you cut because it adds analysis but not utility?
- What was missing when trying to make a practical decision?
- Did the entry change what wording you would choose?
- Would you want more entries in this format?

## Evidence Standard for the Review Cycle

The review cycle should produce evidence stronger than generic positive reaction.

What counts:
- specific examples of changed wording or changed judgment;
- specific reuse claims;
- specific confusion points;
- explicit ranking of useful versus unnecessary entry components.

What does not count:
- polite praise;
- abstract endorsement of the project idea;
- agreement with the politics without evidence of practical use.

## Working Conclusion

The lexicon earns continuation not by sounding intelligent but by demonstrating that a small number of users or proxies can make better wording and framing decisions with it than without it.
