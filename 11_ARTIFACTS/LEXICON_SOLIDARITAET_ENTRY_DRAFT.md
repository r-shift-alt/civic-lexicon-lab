## User Job Check

- What real wording, translation, or framing decision is this entry meant to help with?
  - It helps a bilingual political writer, editor, or translator decide whether a German use of `Solidarität` should be rendered directly as `solidarity` or whether the sentence needs a gloss that restores institutional social protection, collective burden-sharing, or labor-linked cohesion.
- Why is direct translation risky or incomplete here?
  - Because `solidarity` is often close in meaning but can sound more activist, mobilizing, or slogan-like in English than `Solidarität` does in German policy-adjacent or union-embedded usage.
- What kind of user would most likely need this entry?
  - Translation-adjacent political communicators, policy writers, editors, researchers, and journalists working between German and English.

## Core Meaning Block

### Source-Language Context
- Concise working definition in source-language political context:
  - In German public language, `Solidarität` commonly names mutual responsibility, collective support, and willingness to share burdens, often with a labor, welfare-state, or social-cohesion register rather than only a protest or slogan register.

### Target-Language Context
- Concise working definition in target-language political context:
  - In English public language, `solidarity` commonly names active alignment, support, or collective commitment, especially in labor, movement, crisis, or justice-oriented settings.

### Overlap Summary
- What is genuinely shared across both terms?
  - Both terms can describe collective support, mutual obligation, and willingness to bear costs with or for others in labor, crisis, or public-interest contexts.

### Mismatch Summary
- What differs in baggage, institutional meaning, moral tone, or common usage?
  - The main mismatch is not basic meaning but register. German `Solidarität` is more comfortable inside organized labor, social protection, and social-cohesion language that can sound routine or institutionally embedded. English `solidarity` often sounds more like explicit alignment, mobilization, or moral appeal.

## Framing Block

- Why is the term politically contested?
  - Because it can be invoked as a virtue, a labor principle, a justification for redistribution or social protection, or a rhetorical demand for alignment. Different actors treat it either as a durable institutional obligation or as a call to action.
- What framing baggage is common in German usage?
  - Labor movement history, collective burden-sharing, welfare-state justification, and a broader sense of social connectedness plus reciprocal obligation.
- What framing baggage is common in English usage?
  - Labor and movement language, public alignment with a cause or group, and a more overtly exhortative tone.
- When is literal translation safe?
  - When the German source is clearly mobilizing, labor-forward, crisis-oriented, or morally declarative, and the English sentence also wants that tone.
- When is literal translation misleading?
  - When `Solidarität` is doing quieter work around institutionalized mutual obligation, social protection, or routinized social cohesion and a plain `solidarity` rendering makes the sentence sound thinner or more slogan-like than the source.
- Is there a false-equivalence warning that should be stated explicitly?
  - Yes. `Solidarity` is usually the closest rendering, but it should not be treated as a perfect one-to-one equivalent when the German source is using `Solidarität` to justify organized social obligation rather than to signal overt political alignment.

## Guidance Block

- Recommended rendering in policy context:
  - Use `solidarity` if the surrounding sentence already makes the institutional setting clear. If not, use `solidarity` with a restoring phrase such as `social solidarity`, `solidarity-based burden-sharing`, or a short gloss that names collective protection or social obligation.
- Recommended rendering in media or public-commentary context:
  - Use `solidarity` directly for movement, labor, or crisis rhetoric. In explanatory writing, consider adding a brief modifier or appositive if the German source is clearly invoking social-state or collective-protection logic.
- When should the original term be preserved and glossed?
  - Preserve `Solidarität` and add a gloss when the term itself is under discussion, when the German political register matters, or when a literal translation would hide the social-state or labor-embedded layer.
- When is paraphrase better than direct translation?
  - When the sentence depends less on the slogan value of `Solidarität` and more on the underlying idea of mutual social obligation, collective protection, or burden-sharing.
- Reusable warning sentence for writers or translators:
  - If the German source uses `Solidarität` to justify social protection, collective burden-sharing, or union-linked cohesion, plain `solidarity` may be accurate but still too movement-coded unless the surrounding sentence restores the institutional context.
