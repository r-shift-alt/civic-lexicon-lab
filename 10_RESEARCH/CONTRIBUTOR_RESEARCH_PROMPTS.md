# Contributor Research Prompts

## Purpose
This note turns the broader internal research agenda into contributor-facing prompts that are directly useful for the current artifact track.

The goal is not to open an unlimited research program.
The goal is to help contributors find evidence, examples, and bounded insights that improve the first lexicon entries.

## How to Use This Note
A good contribution should usually do at least one of the following:
- provide a strong German or English source example;
- clarify a real translation mismatch;
- identify framing baggage that a literal rendering would hide;
- show where an entry is too vague, too broad, or too theory-heavy;
- improve the practical guidance a writer or translator could reuse.

Please optimize for traceable, bounded, reviewable inputs.

## Highest-Priority Prompts for the Current Pilot Concepts

### 1. Solidarität / solidarity
Look for examples or notes that help answer:
- In which German political or policy contexts does `Solidarität` carry welfare-state, labor, or social-obligation meaning that English `solidarity` only partly captures?
- In which English contexts does `solidarity` sound movement-oriented, activist, or moral in a way that shifts the tone away from ordinary German institutional usage?
- Are there cases where literal translation works well, and cases where it clearly misleads?
- What short warning would actually help a writer choose between direct translation and contextual gloss?

Helpful source types:
- party texts, union language, speeches, policy commentary, serious journalism.

### 2. soziale Marktwirtschaft / social market economy
Look for examples or notes that help answer:
- Which German sources show `soziale Marktwirtschaft` as a historically loaded institutional term rather than a generic label for capitalism with safety nets?
- When English `social market economy` is used, does it preserve the German baggage or flatten it into vague explanation?
- What misunderstanding appears most often when the phrase is translated literally?
- What guidance would help a writer decide when to translate literally, when to gloss, and when to explain the term explicitly?

Helpful source types:
- party programs, government materials, comparative policy writing, explanatory journalism.

### 3. Staatsbürgerschaft / citizenship / Bürgersinn
Look for examples or notes that help answer:
- Which German uses are clearly about legal membership or formal citizenship?
- Which nearby uses drift toward civic virtue, belonging, or public-mindedness, where `Bürgersinn` may be the more relevant concept?
- Where does English `citizenship` fail to cover the relevant nuance?
- What would a practical warning look like for writers who might flatten legal status and civic ethos into one English term?

Helpful source types:
- government and parliamentary texts, citizenship-law discussion, civic education material, serious commentary.

## General Research Prompts for the Lexicon Format

### A. Translation mismatch
- Which politically contested German terms appear easy to translate into English but become misleading in real use?
- Which English renderings are common but strategically weak because they erase institutional or rhetorical context?

### B. Framing baggage
- Where does a term carry different ideological, historical, or emotional baggage across German and English?
- Which source examples make that baggage visible quickly?

### C. Practical guidance
- What kind of note would actually help someone making a live writing or translation decision?
- Which parts of a draft entry feel reusable, and which parts feel like interesting but unnecessary analysis?

### D. Source quality
- Which public or policy sources are especially good at illustrating consequential usage?
- Which source classes look tempting but mostly create noise or overcomplicate the entry?

### E. Entry boundaries
- Which concepts seem too broad, too diffuse, or too dependent on adjacent theory to belong in version one?
- Which concepts produce high value with relatively bounded research effort?

## Good Contribution Shapes

### Option 1: Source bundle
Provide:
- concept name
- 1 to 3 German source examples
- 1 to 3 English source examples
- short note on why each example matters
- any likely mismatch or false-equivalence risk

### Option 2: Translation warning note
Provide:
- the term pair
- the common literal rendering
- why it is sometimes misleading
- one or two contexts where a gloss or paraphrase is better

### Option 3: Entry critique
Provide:
- what part of a draft or template is useful
- what part is too abstract or too dense
- what a practitioner would need in order to act

## What Not to Optimize For
Please do not optimize for:
- broad ideology synthesis;
- manifesto writing;
- exhaustive literature reviews detached from the pilot entries;
- expanding to many languages at once;
- replacing traceable examples with intuition alone.

## Review Standard
A strong research contribution should make at least one of these outcomes more likely:
- the first three entries become easier to draft;
- the entries become more credible;
- the guidance becomes more practically usable;
- the project avoids a false equivalence or scope mistake early.

## Working Conclusion
The current research question is narrow:
what evidence and practical judgment will help the first lexicon entries become genuinely usable for people working between German and English?

Contributions that sharpen that question are high-value.
Contributions that reopen the whole architecture are not.
