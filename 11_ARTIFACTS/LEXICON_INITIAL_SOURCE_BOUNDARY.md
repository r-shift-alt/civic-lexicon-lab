# Lexicon Initial Source Boundary

## Purpose

This document defines the minimum source boundary for the 8 concept version of the German-English lexicon MVP.

The boundary is meant to keep the first entries evidence-based without turning each concept into open-ended discourse research.

## Source-Boundary Principle

Use a deliberately narrow but credible source mix.

The source boundary should be wide enough to show real public or policy usage, but narrow enough that completing 8 entries remains realistic with manual work.

For version one, the goal is not exhaustive coverage. The goal is traceable, reviewable examples that expose real translation and framing mismatch.

## Allowed Source Classes

Preferred source classes for version one:
- party programs or official party texts;
- government or parliamentary materials;
- speeches, statements, or interviews by major political actors;
- major newspaper and magazine usage;
- think-tank, NGO, foundation, or policy-institute reports;
- high-quality explanatory commentary when the wording itself is illustrative.

## Excluded or De-Prioritized Source Classes

Do not rely primarily on:
- dictionaries alone;
- random social media posts;
- informal forum chatter;
- purely academic debates that do not connect to practical public usage;
- secondary summaries with no traceable underlying usage.

These sources may help orientation, but they should not carry the entry.

## Minimum Evidence Boundary Per Entry

Each entry should aim for:
- at least 1 German usage example from a public, policy, or political context;
- at least 1 English usage example from a comparable context if feasible;
- at least 1 source that helps clarify institutional or framing baggage;
- enough metadata that another reviewer can trace the example quickly.

## Recommended Shared Source Pool

To reduce research sprawl, version one should draw repeatedly from a shared pool rather than a bespoke universe for every term.

### German-side shared pool
- Bundestag or federal government materials;
- party programs or party principle papers from major parties;
- major German newspapers or magazines;
- selected speeches or interviews by cabinet members, party leaders, or other visible actors.

### English-side shared pool
- UK or US parliamentary, government, or major party materials where relevant;
- major English-language newspapers or magazines;
- speeches, policy papers, and commentary from institutions with real political influence;
- selected think-tank or NGO materials when they illustrate common usage well.

## Comparative Rule

Do not force exact institutional equivalence between the German and English source environments.

The aim is not to prove that the two political systems are identical.
The aim is to compare how a contested term functions in serious public usage across the two language contexts.

## Entry Research Limit

To avoid drift, each entry should begin with a bounded research pass:
- 2 to 4 promising German sources;
- 2 to 4 promising English sources;
- 1 short synthesis pass by the project.

If an entry cannot become credible within that boundary, treat it as a warning sign.
Either the term is too diffuse for version one or the entry design needs tightening.

## Source Prioritization Rule

When choosing sources, prefer:
1. sources that show the term used in a consequential public context;
2. sources that reveal framing baggage clearly;
3. sources that can be understood and checked by a reviewer without specialist access;
4. sources that are reusable across multiple entries.

## Concept-Level Guidance

### Solidarität / Solidarity
Look for:
- welfare-state or labor-context usage in German;
- movement, social-justice, or labor-context usage in English;
- examples where moral tone overlaps but political baggage differs.

### soziale Marktwirtschaft / social market economy
Look for:
- German institutional and party usage;
- English explanatory or comparative usage;
- examples showing that literal translation often hides context-specific history.

### Umverteilung / redistribution
Look for:
- tax, welfare, and inequality framing;
- contrast between technocratic and moralized usage;
- examples from mainstream policy argument rather than fringe polemic.

### Sozialstaat / welfare state
Look for:
- German constitutional, policy, or party usage;
- English-language usage where welfare state carries different ideological charge;
- examples that show institutional overlap but rhetorical mismatch.

### Staatsbürgerschaft / citizenship / Bürgersinn
Look for:
- legal membership usage versus civic-virtue usage;
- examples showing that one English term may not cover all German civic nuance;
- places where glossing is better than flattening.

### Integration / Integration
Look for:
- migration and social-cohesion contexts;
- examples showing whether the term sounds administrative, moral, or assimilationist;
- contexts where direct translation hides political baggage.

### Sicherheit / security
Look for:
- public safety, social security, and geopolitical contexts;
- examples showing semantic width differences;
- cases where English requires disambiguation.

### Freiheit / freedom
Look for:
- constitutional, civic, and economic rhetoric;
- differences between moral symbolism and policy usage;
- examples showing where freedom carries broader or narrower political resonance.

## Stop Rule

If source gathering repeatedly pushes an entry toward:
- deep historiography;
- extensive ideology mapping;
- or large-volume corpus work,
stop and ask whether the term is still suitable for version one.

## Working Conclusion

Version one should use a small shared evidence ecology, not a custom research universe for each term. The source boundary is credible when it supports practical guidance and traceable examples, not when it exhausts the literature.
